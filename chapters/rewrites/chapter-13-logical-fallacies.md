# Chapter 13: Logical Fallacies

An argument can be fueled by conviction and delivered with force, yet remain entirely hollow. The weight of a voice says nothing about the truth of its words. Logical fallacies are the structural fractures in reasoning that allow a broken argument to sound whole. They appear in politics, in advertising, and in the desperate defenses I build when I am losing ground.

To think critically is to weigh a claim by its substance. It is the cold evaluation of evidence, the logical bridge between premise and conclusion, and the endurance of the logic under pressure. A strong argument presents evidence, connects it to a conclusion through valid logic, and survives challenge. A fallacy breaks those links. It offers a substitute for evidence, makes an invalid logical leap, or survives only because nobody called it out.

The aim is not to become a technician of debate who kills conversations with terminology. The aim is to recognize when a claim is built on sand, regardless of who is speaking. Often, when I feel the heat of defensiveness or the pull of emotion, it is because the reasoning has already failed and the error has gone unnamed.

These are the common blueprints of a broken thought. These are the patterns I encounter most often, and the ones I am most likely to use when I am not watching my own hands.

---

## Ad Hominem

I attack the man because I cannot break the argument.

This is the emergency exit for a failing mind. When I cannot refute the words, I turn my blade on the speaker. I target their history, their character, or their motives. None of this changes the truth of what was said. A poisoned source can still offer a clean fact. A saint can be a fool.

I have used this. When my beliefs are cornered and I lack a valid answer, the instinct is to disqualify the messenger. I tell myself I am being thorough, but I am merely retreating behind a counterattack. The argument stands or falls on its own strength. The person delivering it is irrelevant to the logic.

## Straw Man

I fabricate a weak imitation of an argument so I can tear it apart.

The straw man is a monument to distortion. I take what was said and stretch, simplify, or exaggerate it just enough that it becomes easy to knock down. Then I claim victory over the original thought. I haven't defeated their position; I've defeated a version of the argument I invented.

This is a theft of truth, even when it happens by mistake. When the stakes are high and my ego is on the line, the distortion is rarely an accident. I must be willing to state the strongest version of the opposing case. If I can only win against a hollow shell, I have proven nothing.

## False Dichotomy

I present two paths as if the world has no others.

"You are either with us or against us." These frames kill the nuance and the alternatives that complicate the narrative. They force a binary choice upon a reality that is not binary. I am most vulnerable when I accept the frame as it is given, moving into the trap without looking for the exit.

Once I agree that there are only two positions, I am living inside a lie. I have to ask who narrowed the field and what was erased to make the choice look this simple. To accept a forced choice is to ignore the infinite space between the poles.

## Circular Reasoning

The conclusion is buried inside the premise.

I claim a thing is true simply because it is true. I tell myself I am right because I would never believe something that is wrong. The argument offers no evidence beyond its own reflection; it merely loops back to the start. The verdict is assumed before the trial begins, then restated as if a victory were won.

This is hardest to detect in my own mind because it feels like conviction. I believe something so entirely that the feeling itself feels like proof. But a strong belief is not a fact. I must be able to point to something outside my own certainty to justify the claim. If the only evidence for my thought is the thought itself, I am trapped in a room with no doors.

## Appeal to Emotion

I use the heat of feeling to mask a lack of fact.

Emotion is a signal that something matters, but it is not a substitute for evidence. When feeling is used to replace proof rather than support it, the reasoning fails. This is often a tool to silence dissent; the suggestion is that to question the claim is to betray the value it claims to protect. It is a shield used to stop a conversation that should be happening.

I have leaned on this when my position was thin. The temptation is to raise the stakes until the disagreement looks like a moral defect in the other person. That is not persuasion; it is a siege. If I must make someone feel shame to win the point, my argument is too weak to survive on its own merits.

## Appeal to Authority

I cite the speaker to avoid the labor of proving the speech.

A title is not a fact. An expert in one field is a novice in another, and even the master can be wrong. When I lead with credentials instead of logic, I am demanding trust rather than inviting scrutiny. I am asking for a bypass. I am trying to skip the trial the argument is required to survive.

I have hidden behind this. A reputation or a consensus can feel like a solid floor, but it is often just a thin layer of prestige. Expertise might lower the chance of a mistake, but it never eliminates the possibility of failure. The argument must still hold its own weight. If my only defense is that someone important agrees with me, I do not yet understand the truth well enough to speak it.

## Whataboutism

"You're criticizing this, but what about that?"

This tactic sidesteps the issue by dragging in a comparison. It does not address whether the original claim is true; it merely redirects the pressure. Two separate problems do not cancel each other out. Pointing to a second wrong does not wash away the first. It is a pivot designed to ensure that no one is held accountable.

This is the primary tool of bad-faith defense. I must recognize the turn and refuse to follow it. If a specific point is met with a redirection, I have to name it and return to the original question. A valid comparison can be addressed on its own time, but it does not get to take the place of the truth at hand.

## Tu Quoque

I dismiss the argument by pointing to the hypocrisy of the person making it.

This is the "you also" defense. I reject a valid argument because the person speaking has a history of doing the opposite. I tell myself that if they cannot follow their own advice, the advice must be flawed. It is a specific type of ad hominem that uses hypocrisy as a shield. I disqualify the source to avoid engaging with the substance of the claim.

The messenger and the message are separate entities. A person can be a hypocrite and still be right. Their personal failure to meet a standard does not change the validity of the standard itself. To find the truth, I must look at the logic, not the flaws of the person holding it. If I only listen to the perfect, I will never hear anything at all.

## Special Pleading

I demand a law for the world and a sanctuary for myself.

This is the myth of the one true exception. I hold the line for everyone else, yet I find a thousand reasons why I am allowed to cross it. My situation always feels unique, my reasons always feel heavy, my failures always feel justified. But if a standard only applies when it doesn't hurt, it is not a standard. It is a weapon I choose to put down whenever it points at me.

The air is thin at the top of this delusion. The test is simple: would I accept this excuse from a stranger? If the answer is no, then I am not special. I am merely a hypocrite seeking a soft place to land. To live with any shred of honor, I must submit to the same gravity I expect the rest of the world to obey.

## Also Worth Recognizing

These appear frequently enough to name on sight.

**Hasty Generalization** — Drawing a broad conclusion from too small a sample.

**Slippery Slope** — Claiming that one event will inevitably lead to a chain of negative outcomes without evidence for each step.

**Appeal to Ignorance** — Arguing that a claim is true because it hasn't been proven false, or false because it hasn't been proven true.

**Red Herring** — Introducing irrelevant information to shift attention away from the actual issue.

**Ad Populum (Bandwagon)** — Arguing that something is true or right because a large number of people believe it.

**Burden of Proof** — Avoiding the obligation to provide evidence for a positive claim by demanding the other side disprove it.

**Anecdotal Evidence** — Using a personal story or isolated example to make a general claim.

**Appeal to Tradition** — Arguing something is right or good because it has always been done that way.

**Cherry-Picking** — Selecting only the evidence that supports a position while ignoring contradicting evidence.

**Loaded Question** — Asking a question that embeds an unjustified assumption, making any answer self-incriminating.

**Black-and-White Fallacy** — Treating a spectrum as if only the two extreme ends exist.

**Appeal to Nature** — Arguing that something is good because it is natural, or bad because it isn't.

**Ad Nauseam** — Repeating a claim until it is accepted as true through sheer repetition.

**False Analogy** — Drawing a comparison between two things that are not similar in the relevant way.

**Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc** — Assuming that because one thing followed another, the first caused the second.

**Poisoning the Well** — Discrediting a person before they have made their argument.

**Moving the Goalposts** — Shifting the criteria for success once the original criteria have been met.

**Relative Privation** — Dismissing a legitimate concern by pointing to worse problems elsewhere.

**Two Wrongs Make a Right** — Justifying a wrong by pointing to a different wrong.

## How to Use This

I do not study these to become a judge of other people's failures. I study them to stay honest with myself.

The goal is not to carry this knowledge as a weapon or to win arguments by disqualifying people. Using logic as a blunt instrument would be its own kind of dishonesty. The vital question is never whether the person across from me is failing; it is whether I am. When the ground begins to give way, the instinct is to reach for a fallacy to save my pride. I must learn to catch that impulse and return to the substance.

However, recognizing these patterns serves a cold, practical purpose when I am on the receiving end. If someone is trying to persuade me, I need to know if I am being given a truth or a hollow substitute. Spotting a fallacy in another’s words is not a verdict on their character. It is a filter.

It allows me to see where the evidence ends and where the noise begins. That distinction matters every time I am asked to make a decision based on someone else’s claims. If I cannot separate the signal from the distraction, I am not thinking. I am just being led.
