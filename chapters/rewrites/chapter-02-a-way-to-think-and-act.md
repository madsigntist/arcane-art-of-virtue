# Chapter 2 — A Way to Think and Act

I didn't start out trying to build a system. I started out trying to understand where I had gone wrong and how to fix it in my life. Destructive ideals and crippling delusions were still fresh in my mind and the need to find a 'truth' was something I desperately needed. I needed this truth to be simple. Not to say that I was looking for something that was easy, easy implies low effort. I needed it simple as in uncomplex. Simple still takes effort, the idea of lifting boulders isn't difficult to understand, but it certainly takes effort. I needed something easy to understand, something that couldn't get muddled up too easily.

Introspection led to self-reflection, the "what" was wrong became more clear and the "why" followed. I was able to begin a rough sketch of a simple idea. While reading a book by Robert Anton Wilson I was introduced to the idea that in order to be truly open minded one should actively try to disprove their own beliefs. So I began to play devil's advocate, filtering out parts of my thinking that didn't hold to scrutiny.

Over time, being both the author and editor of my personal system I began to develop a concern. Arrogance is a tentacle of ego. I had a fear that 'declaring my own truths' could be arrogance masquerading as enlightenment. So I began to explore other schools of thought. To safeguard against a 'god complex' style of thinking. Philosophies are ideas, and I love that, because you can agree with part of an idea and utilize that. Unlike religion where most of the time its an all or nothing relationship. The order in which I was introduced to these ideas was not this neat chronologically, but what I found was the following.

## Nothing Means Anything Unless I Decide It Does

I've never been a religious person. A declared moral authority with defined rules and consequences for breaking them asked me to accept things I had no way to verify. I looked for that authority in the world and couldn't find it. What I found was something more neutral. The universe doesn't appear to be on anyone's side. Good behavior doesn't guarantee good outcomes. Bad behavior isn't reliably punished. Morality, as best I can tell, is something people build, either individually or collectively. It's constructed, not discovered.

That left me with a question. If nothing is written into the fabric of existence by default, what am I supposed to do with that?

The universe doesn't come with instructions. There's no inherent reason for being. No built-in purpose waiting to be found. That idea, at its core, is existentialism. There is no inherent meaning, so any meaning I experience is meaning I've created through my choices.

That could be paralyzing if I stayed there. But I don't. The absence of inherent meaning is not a problem to solve. It's a condition to work within. If nothing means anything by default, then what matters is what I choose to build, how I choose to act, and whether those choices hold up under honest scrutiny.

'What is the meaning of life?' is not our question to ask, but our question to answer. And I've answered it for myself. My purpose is to experience joy in all its incalculable causes. Joy found in virtue, in progress, and in experiences. Not happiness in the shallow sense, not comfort, but the kind of satisfaction that comes from living in a way I can respect.

That answer is mine. It's not universal. It doesn't need to be.

## If It Works, It's True

Pragmatism is a simple standard. The truth or value of an idea lies in what it actually does. Not what it claims, not how elegant it sounds. What concrete difference it makes when applied to a real problem is the only question worth asking. If it works consistently, it's true enough.

What I find useful about that framing is that it treats ideas as tools. A hammer is good if it drives a nail. A belief is good if it produces results worth having: resilience, better decisions, stronger relationships. The question isn't "is this philosophically airtight?" The question is "does this work for what I'm trying to solve?"

This changes how I hold beliefs. I don't need to defend them as permanent truths. I need to test them. If I look at the consequences of acting on a belief and they don't hold up, the belief needs revision. That's not weakness. That's how the system stays functional. Knowledge isn't a fixed foundation. It's a working draft, and that draft gets edited as I collect more experience. Any belief I refuse to examine is the only one that can't improve.

The standard I apply to everything in this book is the same. I don't claim these ideas are universally correct or derived from some higher truth. I claim they work. They've worked for me across years of sustained effort, through conditions that tested them in ways I couldn't predict. Not because they're perfect, but because they produce results I can verify through my own behavior and its consequences.

That removes the pressure to be right in any absolute sense. When something stops working, I adjust. Usefulness over dogma.

In order for my beliefs to be strong, I must constantly be trying to disprove them. If they hold up, they're reinforced. If they don't, they need revision. Either outcome is useful. The only wasted position is one I refuse to examine.

## This Is Not Stoicism

I didn't start with stoicism. I hadn't even heard of it when the core of this system was taking shape. What drew me to it later was how much of my own introspection and conclusions were in line with these ancient writings. To be honest, discovering stoicism gave me a guilty bit of validation. Seeing common threads between my personal exploration and these revered Stoics.

Over time my system evolved, and it was absolutely influenced by the stoic writings I read. But it was also influenced by many other things. So saying it's different would be inaccurate. Aspects of stoicism, among other things, are integrated into this design.

But calling it stoicism would also be inaccurate. Stoicism as a philosophy carries a specific framework. It has formal pillars, a defined relationship to fate, an ethics rooted in living according to nature. My system doesn't follow that architecture. It borrows from it where the ideas prove useful and departs where they don't.

The distinction matters because I'm not interested in belonging to a school of thought. I'm interested in building something that works. If an idea from stoicism helps me regulate under stress, I use it. If an idea from somewhere else helps me understand power dynamics or relationships, I use that too. The source matters less than the application.

## What Informed the Thinking

Several schools of thought have shaped how I think, though I don't belong to any of them.

Stoicism, primarily through Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, and Seneca, gave me language for things I was already practicing. Regulate yourself. Focus on what you can control. Accept what you can't. Those ideas get their own chapter later in the book.

A skepticism toward hierarchy and authority runs through how I think about power, systems, and relationships. Compliance should always require justification. No structure earns obedience just by existing. That thread shows up in how I approach community, solidarity, and parenting. Those ideas are explored in the community chapter.

Critical thinking, specifically the study of cognitive biases and logical fallacies, has been essential. Understanding how my own mind distorts perception is just as important as understanding my character defects. Both are covered in the reference chapters toward the end of the book.

These aren't identities I wear. They're lenses I use. The system I've built pulls from whatever proves useful and discards what doesn't.

## Virtue as Practice

Everything in this book comes back to virtue. Virtue is an ideal. A standard of character that is never fully reached, only ever approached. That's not a flaw in the concept. It's the point. Because it can't be completed, the only honest relationship with it is practice. Repeated, deliberate, ongoing effort toward something I know I'll never perfectly hold.

The virtues I practice are defined in the next chapter through the three rules. What matters here is the underlying principle. Virtue is not sustained by insight alone. Understanding the right thing to do is not the same as doing it, and doing it once is not the same as doing it consistently. Repetition is what maintains alignment. A single decision doesn't build character. A pattern of decisions does.

This is what separates a lived virtue ethics from philosophy I've read about but don't practice. Reading about courage doesn't produce it. Practicing courage, choosing to act despite fear over and over again, is what builds it. The same applies to honesty, to selflessness, to composure. These aren't qualities I either have or don't. They're skills developed through repetition and sustained through return.

The practice matters more than the outcome of any single instance. Drift is inevitable. Return is the discipline. The question is never whether I'll fall short of the ideal. The question is whether I come back to it.

Work for the life you want. Or deal with the life you got.
