## Chapter 9: The Commons and the Community

**On Power Structures**

The framing of authority as something that must "justify its stay" draws on a long tradition of political thought that treats authority as contingent, not natural. The specific claim here is not that all authority is illegitimate but that authority without a built-in correction mechanism is a structural defect regardless of who holds the position. This is not a left or right argument. It is an engineering argument. Chomsky's _On Anarchism_ articulates this most cleanly: that any structure of authority must prove its legitimacy or be dismantled.

The idea that power flows toward whoever controls the dependencies — resources, information, access — is something I arrived at through direct observation before I ever read political theory. But Graeber's _Debt: The First 5,000 Years_ gave me the historical depth behind it, showing how debt and obligation have functioned as mechanisms of control across every civilization, not as neutral financial instruments. Herman and Chomsky's _Manufacturing Consent_ confirmed the other half: that the most durable power isn't exercised through force but installed in the frame before any choice is offered. The media doesn't need to lie. It only needs to set the terms of the conversation.

Zinn's _A People's History of the United States_ and Dunbar-Ortiz's _An Indigenous Peoples' History of the United States_ function as companion texts on this point. Both reconstruct the historical record from the position of those on the receiving end of power, not those exercising it. Parenti's _Blackshirts and Reds_ traces how class interests have shaped the alignment of political movements in ways that cut across the obvious ideological lines.

The "kill switch" language is mine. The principle underneath it is old.

**On Class Awareness**

Class is not primarily a conversation about income brackets. It is a conversation about control. Who controls their time. Who must ask permission to leave early. Who can absorb a $500 emergency and who cannot. Who has access to institutional knowledge and who has to figure it out alone.

I did not come to this through academic reading. I came to it through working in systems where the rules were written for someone else and the exits were hidden. The "fish in a toxic lake" metaphor in this chapter is meant to be a diagnostic tool, not an absolution. It does not remove individual responsibility. It repositions it. Before you ask what is wrong with a person, ask what the structure they are operating inside of rewards. Most of the time, that question is more productive.

Kendi's _Stamped from the Beginning_ is the most rigorous account I have read of how this mechanism works in the specific context of race in America: that racist ideas were not the cause of racist policy, but the justification constructed after the fact to protect it. The sequence matters. Policy first, ideology second. Newton's _Revolutionary Suicide_ is where this becomes personal. It is not a political manual. It is a record of someone doing the work — building programs, feeding people, refusing to wait for a system to correct itself.

**On Social Justice**

The chapter deliberately avoids the language of contemporary social justice discourse because that language has become a tribal signal rather than a useful tool. The underlying analytical principle — that structural conditions produce outcomes that cannot be explained by individual behavior alone — is sound regardless of the political packaging.

Whether you are analyzing a corporation, a government agency, a school, or a neighborhood association, the questions are the same: What does the structure reward? What does it protect? Who benefits from the current default? Who is made invisible by it? These are not radical questions. They are the questions a competent manager asks of a failing system. The political charge around them does not change their utility.

Baldwin's _The Fire Next Time_ is the clearest distillation of why the evasion of these questions is not neutral. It is a cost. You can read it as a political text or as a moral one. Either way, it demands the same thing: that you look directly at what is there.

I am not a neutral party on any of this. My views have been shaped by experience, by where I grew up, by the systems I have worked inside. I put the framework in the chapter rather than the conclusions because the framework is transferable and the conclusions are mine. You may reach different ones.

**On Mutual Aid**

The distinction between charity and mutual aid in this chapter is not rhetorical. It is structural.

Charity is vertical. It flows from someone with surplus downward to someone without. It depends on the continued goodwill of the donor and the continued humility of the recipient. It is inherently fragile and often extractive in its own way — it can reinforce dependency, create narratives of saviorhood, and keep power concentrated at the top of the giving relationship. Giridharadas's _Winners Take All_ is the definitive account of this mechanism: how philanthropy by the powerful functions to preserve the conditions that made the giving necessary in the first place.

Mutual aid is horizontal. It operates on the premise that roles are reversible. Today I have the surplus, the skill, or the bandwidth. Tomorrow I may be the one who needs the network. The contribution is not generosity. It is maintenance of a system I depend on. The Black Panther survival programs that Newton describes in _Revolutionary Suicide_ are the clearest historical example of this I have encountered: not waiting for institutions to deliver, but building the delivery mechanism yourself, for the people in front of you.

The actionable items at the end of the chapter are deliberately small and local. Mutual aid does not require a political conversion. It requires a decision to be useful to the people near you.
