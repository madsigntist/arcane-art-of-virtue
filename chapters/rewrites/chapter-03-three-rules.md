# Chapter 3 — Three Rules

I'm sure at some point in my life someone taught me how to act. At least on some basic level. Be kind, don't lie, pleases, and thank yous. All that. I most likely saw contradictions in the lessons being taught and the actions of those teaching the lessons. Do as I say not as I do, or else. Eventually, as so many of us do, I reached a point in my life where the 'or else' was no longer a threat. And in my new found freedom I incorporated the underlying lesson learned, act in self-interest. This was all done on a more subconscious level, at the time I would have adamantly declared my integrity. Even alone in the mirror, I would have been convinced that I was a 'good person'. Even if a deeper introspection of my character and behavior would have exposed the opposite. In all truth, I didn't even have a design or model of what a 'good person' was.

My appetite for self-interest was vast, and lasted many years. The accumulation of selfish and dishonest behavior eventually alienated me from every personal relationship, and at that point the mirror reflected clearly to me the person I had become. I may not yet have had a design for what a 'good person' was, but I had a solid example of a bad one.

Nothing stays the same, nothing is stagnant, everything changes. At this point I could accept the truth of who I had become and revel in it, or I could rearchitect myself. I chose the latter.

I needed core principles to shape my character on, easy to remember and as simple as possible. After examining myself and my past behavior with brutal honesty I was able to decipher my defects distilled down to their purest form. Selfishness and Dishonesty. Exploring that further, I found that the driving force behind those characteristics was Fear. So it stood to reason that the simplest rules to live by would be to not do what I had done. Don't be selfish. Don't be dishonest. Don't make decisions based on fear.

What followed after that became more intricate, but as the foundation, these became the three rules.

## The Rules

### Don't be selfish.

I used to think selfishness was obvious. Taking more than your share, putting yourself first in ways everyone can see. It's not. Most of the time it's quiet. Choosing my own comfort because the alternative takes effort I'd rather not spend. Not lifting my head long enough to see how something affects someone other than me.

Selfishness shows up as two sides of the same problem. The inflated ego and the bruised ego. One takes what it doesn't deserve, the other demands attention for its wounds. Both center on self, and both lead to the same place. I lived in both for years, and by the end of it the people closest to me were still there, but the connection wasn't. I had hollowed out my relationships without ever leaving the room.

The opposite of selfishness is not generosity. It's consideration. The practice of pausing long enough to weigh someone else's well-being before I act. When I started putting that consideration into practice, through service, through genuine concern for the welfare of others, the selfishness lost its grip. Not overnight, but steadily. Practiced repeatedly, that pause stops being deliberate and becomes default.

### Don't be dishonest.

I knew I was a liar. I even glamorized it. I thought of myself as a 'hustler'. I idealized cons as a form of intelligence, getting one over on someone else as proof that I was sharp. Reshaping that thinking was the easy part. It was so obviously wrong that once I saw it clearly, I couldn't unsee it.

What took real work was the subtler dishonesty. Bending details, omitting context, shaping a story to land more favorably. Each instance felt small enough to dismiss. The problem is they accumulate. Every small dishonesty trades trust for temporary relief. I was afraid that being too honest would turn me into a sucker, that it would cost me. That fear kept the habit alive long after I'd dropped the more obvious lies. Most people frame too much honesty as something that will cost you.

Not sure I agree with the premise that honesty has a cost. In my experience honesty has a value far more often than a cost. Dishonesty on the other hand, especially the kind that costs you your word or integrity, has a high cost. A debt that is never fully paid. All relationships are based in trust, and a degradation of that trust never truly gets fully repaired. I can spend the better part of a decade dedicated to honesty, keeping my word even when it may not be immediately beneficial. Showing day after day that I will do what I say and can be trusted. And if I was to engage in something that is even slightly construed as being shady or deceptive, those closest to me would at the very least look at me with a side-eye, wondering if I've somewhat returned to my old ways of hedonism.

But with that decade of dedication to honesty, when my word is given, it is given with weight and evidence. That dedication creates integrity. So when I choose honesty, when I choose to keep my word, or accountability when I fall short of my word, especially when that honesty is not materially or situationally beneficial to me, I reinforce my relationships. Even those that have been drastically decimated from my past behavior and actions.

Honesty starts inside before it goes anywhere else. I can't be honest with anyone if I'm not honest with myself first. Most of the dishonesty I've had to correct wasn't aimed at other people. It was aimed at the mirror. Justifications, rationalizations, the stories I told myself about why my behavior was acceptable. Clear self-perception depends on being willing to confront what I'd rather not see about my own motivations. If that internal honesty isn't in place, everything I present outward is built on a cracked foundation.

### Don't make decisions based on fear.

Fear is a reaction to danger, real or imagined. Most of the time, in day to day life, it's imagined. The anticipation of something unpleasant, not the thing itself. And that anticipation drives decisions that look reasonable on the surface but aren't rooted in anything I actually value.

Fear is the seed. It makes me selfish to protect myself and dishonest to avoid consequence. If I trace any violation of the first two rules back far enough, fear is what I find. That connection gets explored in depth in the next chapter, but even at this level, fear as a driver should be watched closely. When I catch myself rationalizing a selfish or dishonest choice, I ask what I'm afraid of. The answer usually explains the behavior.

Fear is not resolved by eliminating it. The emotion can persist without affecting my actions, so long as I recognize it for what it is and choose deliberately. Courage is not the absence of fear. It's the mental and moral strength to act in its presence. The question is not whether I'm afraid. The question is whether my choice is a product of my values or a symptom of what I'm trying to avoid.

## The Three Virtues

Each rule, when practiced, becomes a virtue.

The rules don't ask me to avoid selfish, dishonest, or fearful behavior. They ask me to recognize those tendencies when they show up and choose to act in the opposite direction. That practice of recognition and deliberate change is what builds the virtue. Not the absence of the defect, but the active pursuit of its counterpart.

Don't be selfish, practiced repeatedly, becomes selflessness. An unselfish, loyal concern for the well-being of others. Not just restraint from taking, but a genuine orientation toward service.

Don't be dishonest, practiced over time, becomes honesty as a default. Free from fraud and deception, internally and externally. Not a policy I enforce on myself, but something that becomes natural through repetition.

Don't make decisions based on fear, applied consistently, becomes courage. The mental and moral strength to venture forward, persevere, and withstand difficulty. Fear may still be present. Courage doesn't require its absence, only that it doesn't dictate my actions.

The distinction matters because virtue is not a destination. It's what happens when I keep recognizing where I fall short and choosing differently. The practice is the virtue.

## Using the Rules

I test these rules before and after I act.

Before I act, I run the decision through all three. Is this selfish? Am I being dishonest, with someone else or with myself? Is fear driving this? These checks take seconds. They don't guarantee the right outcome, but they reduce the chance of causing avoidable harm.

After I act, the same three questions work in review. Where was I selfish? Where was I dishonest? What fears influenced my thinking? That review is a daily practice, covered in full in Chapter 10. The practice starts here, with these three questions.

During a violation, the rules provide a path back. I will break them. That is expected. The system is not designed for perfection. It is designed for return.

## Return Over Consistency

I will break these rules. Not occasionally. Regularly. The standard is not rigid adherence. It is the return. Did I catch it? Do I understand what drove it? Am I willing to come back?

A person who breaks a rule and examines why is in a stronger position than a person who follows rules without understanding them. Consistency in the traditional sense, never failing, is not what I'm after. Consistency in return is. That repeated return, done honestly over time, is what preserves character more reliably than rigid compliance ever could.

This is not permission to violate and rationalize. The return has to be honest. If the review is softened, justified, or done for appearances, it stops working. Brutal honesty with myself is the only thing that keeps this system functional.

Three rules. They are not enough to solve every complicated problem I'll face. But they are enough to limit the damage done while I work through them. They don't promise clarity, safety, or success. They give me orientation when certainty is unavailable and pressure is high.

## Truth and Honesty

- Be prepared to be wrong.
- Critically analyze behavior and intentions. Ask why to the end of the line.
- Strive to be critical. Avoid being negative. Analyze merits and faults.
- Acceptance of fault is an opportunity for growth.
- Welcome criticism. It can point out your weak points.
- Find the root of the problem. The source of the trouble. Do not treat the symptoms.
