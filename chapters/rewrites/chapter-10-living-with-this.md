# Chapter 10 — Living With This

Any structure that does not receive regular inspection cannot receive maintenance. Without maintenance, it deteriorates. This is true for buildings, for relationships, for the body. It is also true for character.

Everything I've been building — the rules, the awareness of fear, the regulation, the practice of responding over reacting — requires upkeep. None of it is permanent without attention. I've watched discipline erode in my own life, not through dramatic failure but through gradual neglect. Small skips. Justified exceptions. The drift is quiet enough to miss until the gap between who I want to be and how I'm actually living becomes hard to ignore.

This chapter is about the practice that prevents that drift. Not a destination. A maintenance schedule.

## The Review

The self-review is meant to be simple. A brief, honest assessment of recent behavior. Where was I selfish? Where was I dishonest? What fears are currently occupying my thoughts?

These are the same three questions from the rules, turned inward. They don't require elaborate answers. Short, specific notes are better. The purpose is to surface patterns, not to produce a journal.

A quick review might look like this:

> Selfish: carrying frustrations home, not giving quality time to my family, I can give them more composure, more kindness, more peace
> Dishonest: doing personal tasks on work time
> Fear: finances, debt, professional future, upcoming presentation

These are private. Done in shorthand, meant for no one else. Using a journal app with a passcode, or some other method of protection, makes it easier to write honestly. If there's any concern about the content being seen, the honesty suffers. And without honesty, there's no point.

This only works if the review is brutal. Any attempt to soften, justify, or sanitize what's written defeats the purpose. These notes are not about being kind to myself. They're about being accurate. The drift I described earlier begins precisely where honesty ends.

After the three areas, I add a few things I'm grateful for. Deep or surface level, it doesn't matter.

> Gratitude: time spent with loved ones, progress at the gym, good weather in the morning

This serves as a counterweight. Honest self-review can start to feel like a catalogue of failures if it isn't balanced. Gratitude isn't about positivity. It's about pulling attention toward what is going right, not just what needs fixing. Over time, it trains me to notice those things more often, even outside the review.

If time allows, I take short notes on goals or focal points for the day. Not planning. Not a checklist. A small directional prompt.

The entire practice is designed to be quick. If it took an hour, it would be easy to let slip, and eventually it would fall to the wayside under the weight of daily life. The shorter it is, the more likely it survives a hectic schedule. That matters more than thoroughness.

Reading back through past entries is just as important as writing new ones. A weekly review of previous days often reveals repeating issues — recurring fears, persistent patterns of selfishness, blind spots that only become visible over multiple entries. These patterns are where the most meaningful corrections are made.

Repeated engagement with this practice takes things further than memory. Memorized information has to be recalled with effort. Internalized information operates automatically. The difference is the frequency of return. Through that repetition, I shape my reactions into tailored responses. Reviewing recent behavior helps safeguard against repeating it. I'm more likely to stop in the moment, before I follow through with the action, and change course. Eventually, never taking that less than desirable path to begin with.

The same is true of gratitude. Keeping an eye out for things I'm grateful for trains me to become more aware of what makes life worth living. Stopping and realizing that the moment I'm in is memorable brings a beautiful mixture of elation and peace.

## Forgiveness

Forgiveness is the act of ceasing to feel resentment. That's the whole of it. It doesn't require agreement, reconciliation, or any statement to the other person. It doesn't mean what happened was acceptable. It means I've stopped carrying it.

Resentment costs energy. It occupies attention. It keeps me mentally engaged with a situation I can no longer influence, replaying what was done, rehearsing what I should have said, sustaining a grievance that serves nothing but itself. When I hold resentment, I am not punishing the person who caused it. I am occupying my own mind with them, which is closer to the opposite of what I want.

Understanding the role I play in resentment makes it easier to release. Not every resentment involves my fault. But in many cases, when I look honestly, there is something — selfishness, dishonesty, anger driven by fear. Not always the cause, but often a contributing condition. Naming that doesn't excuse what was done to me. It gives me something actionable instead of something to carry.

In other cases, I had no part at all. Someone caused harm and I'm the one holding the weight of it. In those situations, forgiveness is not about them. It is about what that weight does to me over time and whether I'm willing to keep carrying it.

Forgiveness is a practice, not a moment. Some things don't resolve the first time I try to let them go. They come back. I let them go again. The practice is the repetition, not the perfection.

## Making Amends

Justice is the only way to preserve integrity. When I've caused harm, whether through selfishness, dishonesty, or fear-driven action, the only honest response is to make it right. Not to justify. Not to explain. To compensate.

Making amends is not the same as apologizing. Apologies address what was said. Amends address what was done. The distinction matters because words alone don't repair damage. Behavior does. If I lied, the amend is not saying I'm sorry I lied. It's demonstrating honesty going forward and, where possible, correcting what the lie set in motion.

There are situations where making amends directly would cause more harm. Going to someone with an account of what I've done in order to relieve my own guilt is not an amend. It's self-interest dressed as accountability. In those cases, the amend is in changed behavior and the redirection of effort toward something constructive.

This connects directly to the daily review. The review surfaces where I fell short. Amends is the follow-through. Without it, the review becomes an exercise in noticing patterns I do nothing about.

## Peace, Patience, Persistence, Progress

The chain starts with peace. Peace is composure. It is the regulated state where fear, frustration, and urgency are present but not in control. Without it, nothing that follows is accessible.

Peace gifts me with patience. Patience is not waiting. It is calmness and stability in circumstances that resist resolution. The capacity to stay engaged with something difficult without being consumed by it. When I'm regulated, I can tolerate discomfort without forcing a premature resolution. That tolerance is patience.

Patience gifts me with persistence. The willingness to keep showing up, to repeat the work even when the results aren't visible yet. Persistence is patience in motion. It's the difference between enduring something and continuing to act through it.

Persistence gifts me with progress. Not sudden transformation, but the steady development that comes from sustained effort. Becoming better than I was is not dramatic. Most of it looks like small, repeated corrections compounding over time.

And progress gifts me with gratitude. When I can see how far I've come, even slightly, the urgency fades. Desire, in the sense of constant dissatisfaction, loses its hold. Gratitude resolves desire. Not by eliminating ambition, but by making the present adequate while still moving forward.

This chain matters because it corrects the impulse to rush change. Impatience leads to frustration, frustration leads to quitting, and quitting erases whatever progress was underway. Peace is the entry point. It makes the rest of the chain possible.

## Conscience

Conscience is the internal sense of right and wrong. It is not mystical. It is not a voice from somewhere outside. It is the accumulated result of practiced observation and honest self-review.

The more I review my behavior honestly, the sharper this sense becomes. It is not something I was born with in any developed form. It was built through repetition. Through each review, each identified pattern, each time I recognized that fear or selfishness was driving something I tried to justify, the standard became more clear.

Conscience is what makes the daily review productive. It's the part of me that can tell the difference between an honest assessment and a rationalization. Without it, the review would just be writing. With it, the review becomes correction.

Courage and conscience function together. Conscience identifies what needs to change. Courage is the willingness to act on it. Neither is useful without the other. Seeing clearly and then doing nothing differs very little from not seeing at all.

## Progress in Every Direction

Everything is in motion. That includes me, and it includes the areas of my life that I stop giving attention to.

Progress is not guaranteed to be positive. Health progresses in both directions. So do relationships. So does discipline. A body that isn't maintained doesn't stay neutral. It deteriorates. A mind that isn't challenged narrows. A relationship that isn't cared for erodes. The system I've been building requires upkeep across multiple areas, not just character. And neglect in any one of them doesn't produce a freeze. It produces decline.

Physical progress is straightforward. Exercise, rest, nutrition. A body that is neglected undermines everything else. It's harder to regulate under stress when I'm exhausted, malnourished, or sedentary. Physical condition directly influences emotional regulation, which directly influences composure.

Mental progress is the continued effort to learn, to read, to think about things that extend beyond my immediate concerns. Curiosity is not a luxury. It keeps the mind flexible and reduces the kind of narrow thinking that fear produces. Learning something from everyone, treating the world as full of teachers, is part of this. It keeps me from becoming rigid.

Social progress is the care of relationships. Not in terms of maintaining appearances, but in terms of genuine connection. The ability to communicate honestly, listen fully, and remain present with others requires its own form of practice.

Character progress is the ongoing work of alignment. The daily review, the return to principles. This is where identity is shaped deliberately rather than reactively. A commitment to examining what I value and whether my behavior reflects it.

I control me. That's it. And because nothing holds still on its own, the choice is not whether to progress but in which direction. Give energy to positive progress, or watch things move the other way.

## The Long Game

Be strict with yourself and tolerant with others. This doesn't mean accepting behavior that causes harm. It means holding myself to the highest standard while recognizing that other people are fighting battles I may know nothing about. The standard I apply to myself is mine. Applying it to others is judgment, not discipline.

Put every impression to the test. Not every thought is useful. Not every feeling is accurate. Not every initial reaction reflects what's actually happening. Testing impressions means pausing before treating them as true. It means bringing directed attention to what might otherwise pass through as certainty.

This is a long game. There is no point where I arrive and stop. No version of this where I've done enough work to stop working. The daily review continues. The regulation continues. The correction continues. That might sound exhausting. It isn't. It's the most sustainable thing I've found. When the system is running, when the review is consistent and the awareness is active, life functions with less friction. Not because circumstances improve, but because the internal noise quiets down enough to see them clearly.

Be mindful of when I am not angry, not upset, not anxious. Those moments teach something that struggle doesn't. They show what calm actually feels like, and that feeling becomes something I can return to deliberately. Awareness of the absence of suffering is just as useful as awareness of its presence.

## Orientation

- Be mindful of when you are not angry, upset, etc. and you will learn how to abandon negative feelings.
- The formula is: does it give you energy or drain you. Pursue what gives and does not take.
- Assume formlessness. Be adaptable. Open minded. Tolerant.
- Strategy is about multiplying your options.
- Value intellectual wealth.
- Focus on fundamentals.
- Fundamentals are the first thing learned and the last thing mastered.
- Disorganization doubles the workload.
- No matter your victories, everyone can fail. Do not rest on your laurels.
